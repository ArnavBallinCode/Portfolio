## Overview

My GSoC project began with a practical problem: making live events accessible across languages without requiring interpreters or listeners to install specialist software. I worked on VoxBento’s browser-based interpretation infrastructure, speech-processing pipeline, and integration with the wider Eventyay ecosystem.

The work expanded into a second substantial engineering effort: establishing Eventyay Business, from the plugin scaffold and commercial data model to pricing, subscriptions, invoicing, and controlled billing migration. Alongside implementation, I reviewed contributions across FOSSASIA repositories and helped integrate changes without losing the platform’s permission, migration, and compatibility guarantees.

This report separates those responsibilities into **the official VoxBento project**, **additional Eventyay Business and core work**, and **cross-repository maintenance**. The activity totals describe FOSSASIA work during the reporting window; they are not all attributed to the original GSoC proposal.

## VoxBento: the live interpretation system

### Keep live audio independent of AI processing

I implemented browser WHIP publishing into MediaMTX, made that the primary ingest path, and moved the interpreter portal to FastAPI and WebSocket coordination. This established a clear boundary: the media server transports interpreter audio; Python manages sessions, roles, booth state, and asynchronous processing.

The distinction matters when speech recognition or translation slows down. An AI worker should not sit in the critical path between an interpreter’s microphone and a listener. The early relay implementation also addressed interpreter handoff: keeping the outgoing stream briefly alive while the next publisher connects avoids tearing down the listener’s HLS stream.

**Merged evidence:** [browser WHIP publishing #56](https://github.com/fossasia/voxbento/pull/56), [primary MediaMTX ingest #78](https://github.com/fossasia/voxbento/pull/78), and [FastAPI migration and interpreter relay #81](https://github.com/fossasia/voxbento/pull/81). These establish the implementation history; they are not a benchmark of current end-to-end latency.

### Turn floor audio into captions and translation

I added the floor-audio path: a headless browser joins the Jitsi conference, captures its audio, and streams it through RTSP into MediaMTX. Transcription workers generate segments, translation workers process finalized text, and WebSockets deliver results to listeners. Room and booth configuration lets organizers select providers and target languages; transcripts and translations persist for later access.

This extended the platform beyond human interpreter booths to captions and machine-assisted multilingual access to the main session. [Floor capture, transcription, and translation #159](https://github.com/fossasia/voxbento/pull/159) links the implementation, architecture, configuration, and demonstration material.

### Make the system usable under real operating conditions

I worked on playback and operational failures as well as features. A client-side audio scheduler buffers synthesized speech; model-download states reach the interface instead of appearing as unexplained translation timeouts; unwanted capture and comfort-noise artifacts were removed. Security work added production secret validation, OAuth throttling, token-log redaction, and lifecycle auditing.

**Merged evidence:** [playback and translation reliability #411](https://github.com/fossasia/voxbento/pull/411), [production secret validation #223](https://github.com/fossasia/voxbento/pull/223), and [OAuth security and observability #379](https://github.com/fossasia/voxbento/pull/379).

## Eventyay Business: from foundation to billing

### Establish the plugin and its data model

I built the initial plugin scaffold and introduced `Tier`, `TierVersion`, `TierPrice`, and `TierEntitlement`. These separate a commercial plan from its published configuration, currency and billing interval, and the capabilities it grants. The administration workflow makes published versions immutable and creates a new draft when a plan changes, preserving historical configuration.

I then added the capability registry and organizer subscriptions, including automatic Free-tier assignment. This provides the underlying model for pricing and access rather than embedding plan names throughout the application.

**Merged evidence:** [scaffold #28](https://github.com/fossasia/eventyay-business/pull/28), [foundational models #31](https://github.com/fossasia/eventyay-business/pull/31), [versioned publication workflow #34](https://github.com/fossasia/eventyay-business/pull/34), [capability registry #35](https://github.com/fossasia/eventyay-business/pull/35), and [subscriptions #37](https://github.com/fossasia/eventyay-business/pull/37).

### Keep commercial policy outside Eventyay core

The matching core work introduced a thin entitlement API and a generic provider interface. Feature code asks whether an organizer may use a capability; a plugin provides the decision. Core does not need a direct dependency on Business tier models. With no restricting provider, the entitlement interface preserves the unrestricted self-hosted behavior.

Usage events feed an organizer-scoped, database-enforced idempotency mechanism. A retried usage event must not become a second billable record. Core integration also connects free registrations, bulk email usage, and administrator-seat checks to that interface.

**Merged evidence:** [entitlement kernel #5441](https://github.com/fossasia/eventyay/pull/5441), [provider decisions #5447](https://github.com/fossasia/eventyay/pull/5447), [idempotent usage records #43](https://github.com/fossasia/eventyay-business/pull/43), and [core usage and limits #5505](https://github.com/fossasia/eventyay/pull/5505).

### Build pricing and the subscription lifecycle

I implemented Stripe Checkout and webhook integration for subscriptions and add-ons, then added upgrades, scheduled downgrades, proration, and grace-period handling. The public pricing page became configurable rather than a separate hardcoded view of the offering.

These were steps in an evolving billing architecture. The initial subscription integration is part of the history; the later consolidated-invoice work establishes a different collection boundary and migration controls.

**Merged evidence:** [Checkout and webhooks #77](https://github.com/fossasia/eventyay-business/pull/77), [plan changes #78](https://github.com/fossasia/eventyay-business/pull/78), and [public pricing #146](https://github.com/fossasia/eventyay-business/pull/146).

### Make invoices reproducible and payment ownership explicit

The consolidated billing work reuses Core’s canonical organizer customer and payment method, records authorization for off-session collection, and separates invoice calculation from payment collection. I added auditable invoices, reproducible calculation, and collection through Stripe PaymentIntents.

A key migration concern was preventing the legacy collector and the new billing component from charging the same organizer. Core now exposes a generic ownership signal; Business claims ownership only at an explicit cutover boundary. Preflight checks assess customer, payment method, authorization, and competing legacy subscriptions before activation.

**Merged evidence:** [canonical payer and authorization #105](https://github.com/fossasia/eventyay-business/pull/105), [auditable invoices #106](https://github.com/fossasia/eventyay-business/pull/106), [reproducible calculation #107](https://github.com/fossasia/eventyay-business/pull/107), [PaymentIntent collection #108](https://github.com/fossasia/eventyay-business/pull/108), [core collection ownership #6499](https://github.com/fossasia/eventyay/pull/6499), and [migration preflight #110](https://github.com/fossasia/eventyay-business/pull/110).

### Verify before activating collection

I built a reconciliation mode that executes invoice calculation inside a rollback-only transaction and persists a comparison snapshot. Administrators can inspect results, export them, and explicitly accept a ready result. Acceptance records the exact fingerprint; changed calculations invalidate the earlier acceptance. The activation path rechecks readiness under a lock.

This is a useful example of the architecture behind the UI: **calculate → inspect → accept → activate**, with billing effects kept out of reporting mode. The merged changes include regression tests for calculation side effects, acceptance, and blocked-organizer behavior.

**Merged evidence:** [non-collecting reconciliation #127](https://github.com/fossasia/eventyay-business/pull/127), [review and acceptance #128](https://github.com/fossasia/eventyay-business/pull/128), and [controlled rollout #130](https://github.com/fossasia/eventyay-business/pull/130).

The history also includes [automatic preparation #141](https://github.com/fossasia/eventyay-business/pull/141), which was [reverted in #142](https://github.com/fossasia/eventyay-business/pull/142). It is included in the authored-PR ledger but is **not presented as a retained capability**. Merged PR totals alone cannot describe the final state of a system.

## Maintenance and review work

Reviewing was a substantial part of my contribution. It included checking authorization across repository boundaries, following changes through migrations and integration tests, and requesting fixes before accepting an implementation.

Three examples show the substance behind the review totals:

- **Access rules across Eventyay and VoxBento.** In [Eventyay Interpretation #146](https://github.com/fossasia/eventyay-interpretation/pull/146#pullrequestreview-5647621692), I identified a fallback that could grant interpreted-audio access to someone holding a paid non-admission product, even when Core denied video access. The review asks the plugin to use the authoritative Core rule and add regression coverage.
- **Concurrency and resource lifetime.** In [VoxBento #694](https://github.com/fossasia/voxbento/pull/694#pullrequestreview-5558121677), I requested lifecycle-safe cleanup for a per-booth lock registry and corrected documentation of conditional authentication. The concern was both unbounded retained locks and preserving mutual exclusion during cleanup.
- **Integration without losing security behavior.** In [VoxBento #810](https://github.com/fossasia/voxbento/pull/810#pullrequestreview-5633689729), I requested preservation of queued notifications and the shared listener-safe broadcast path during a rebase. The [follow-up approval](https://github.com/fossasia/voxbento/pull/810#pullrequestreview-5647809691) records the integration fixes and validation.

These examples are evidence of review activity, not a claim that every reviewed PR was merged or that every requested change is complete. Review submissions are counted separately from distinct PRs and from authored implementations.

## Engineering lessons

**Define ownership before extending behavior.** In VoxBento, media transport and AI processing need separate responsibilities. In Eventyay Business, commercial policy belongs behind a provider interface, while collection ownership must be explicit during migration. Both prevent one subsystem’s behavior from silently changing another’s guarantees.

**Retries need durable identities.** A usage event, invoice calculation, or notification can be retried. Database constraints, source references, and reconciliation fingerprints make repeated operations inspectable and help prevent duplicate effects.

**A successful request is not sufficient validation.** Permission fallbacks, lock cleanup, provider failures, and legacy collection paths require tests that exercise the failure boundary. The linked reviews and migration changes show why checking only the happy path misses consequential defects.

**Integration work needs attribution.** Preserving another contributor’s commits, resolving migrations, and closing review findings are valuable work. Describing them accurately makes the record clearer than claiming ownership of every line in an authored PR.
